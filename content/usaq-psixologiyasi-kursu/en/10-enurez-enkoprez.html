Topic 10 · Physiological-Emotional Support

Enuresis/Encopresis Program

A 10-session clinically grounded program — working with nighttime/daytime bladder control difficulties and stool-withholding difficulties.

1

Scientific Definition: Enuresis and Encopresis

Clinical definition: According to the DSM-5-TR, Enuresis is repeated (twice a week for 3 months) involuntary voiding of urine into the bed or clothing, in a child older than 5, with no organic cause (the nighttime type is most common). Encopresis is repeated passage of feces into inappropriate places (clothing) in a child older than 4, with no organic cause (e.g., Hirschsprung's disease ruled out), often associated with chronic constipation (retentive encopresis). Both conditions absolutely require a pediatric/urological examination first, to rule out organic causes.
In simple terms: These aren't 'laziness' or 'carelessness' — they're a biological process, often with a genetic component, in which the connection between the body's sphincter muscles and nervous system hasn't yet fully matured. The child isn't doing this 'on purpose.'

Enuresis shows a familial pattern — if one of the parents had it in the past, the likelihood in the child increases significantly. This is important information for reducing the family's sense of guilt.

Working in this field over the years, I've seen that the greatest harm doesn't come from the physiological problem itself, but from the shame attached to it. When a child begins to see bedwetting as proof of their own 'badness,' this affects self-esteem more lastingly than the problem itself. That's why the first step of every program is to convey, clearly and repeatedly, the message that nothing is wrong with you.

2

Classification of Types

🌙

Nighttime Enuresis

The most common type — deep sleep and the bladder signal not being linked to waking.

☀️

Daytime Enuresis

Less common, often related to bladder function or emotional stress.

🚽

Encopresis (Retentive)

Involuntary passage resulting from stool retention and 'overflow' due to chronic constipation.

3

Multidisciplinary Perspective (5 specialist views)

🧠 Psychological perspective

Shame and loss of self-esteem often cause more harm than the core problem — emotional support is an integral part of treatment.

✋ Occupational therapist perspective

Correct toilet posture (feet supported) helps the pelvic floor muscles function effectively.

🌊 Sensory Integration perspective

A weak interoceptive (internal body) sense can cause delayed recognition of the urination/defecation signal.

🗣️ Speech-language perspective

The ability to express a body need verbally early on may require additional support in children with delays.

🏫 Psychopedagogical perspective

It's important to create a permission environment at school where the child can go to the toilet without feeling ashamed.

A team approach is especially important in enuresis and encopresis, because no single specialist can see the whole picture — the pediatrician rules out organic causes, the psychologist reduces the emotional burden, the occupational therapist optimizes physical mechanics; when all work together, the outcome is faster and more lasting.

4

Focus by Age Group

👶 5-7 years

  • Nighttime enuresis at this age can still be within the clinical norm
  • Fluid regimen and reward systems are the first steps

🎨 7-10 years

  • The bell-and-pad system becomes an effective treatment option
  • Shame increases, emotional support becomes a priority

📚 10+ years

  • Impact on social functioning (camp, sleepovers) is a major source of concern
  • The child's own active involvement in the treatment process is important

Considering the age factor matters for both clinical expectations and choice of intervention — a wait-and-see strategy is often sufficient in the 5-7 age range, while active intervention (bell-and-pad system) is more recommended after age 7, since social-emotional impact starts to become significant at this age.

Core Model: The Bell-and-Pad System and Constipation Management

ConditionCore methodHow it works
Nighttime enuresisBell-and-pad systemA sensor detecting the first drop of moisture triggers a bell, waking the child — over time, the brain links the bladder signal to waking up.
Nighttime enuresisFluid regimen adjustmentGradually reducing fluid intake in the second half of the day.
EncopresisConstipation cleanout (under pediatric supervision)First clearing retained stool, then maintaining consistently soft stool through a fiber-rich diet and fluid regimen.
EncopresisScheduled toilet timeA consistent 5-10 minute toilet sit after meals (using the gastrocolic reflex).

Each method in this chart is evidence-based, but sequence matters — in encopresis, applying behavioral techniques before clearing constipation almost always fails, because as long as the bowel is still full, control isn't physically possible for the child.

Clinical Case: Turan's Story

When working with enuresis, I emphasize to families from the first minute: this isn't a matter of the child's willpower, it's a system in the body that hasn't fully matured yet. Turan was a 7-year-old boy who wet his bed almost every night, and refused birthday parties because he was afraid of sleeping over at kindergarten friends' houses. Pediatric examination ruled out an organic cause, and it turned out his father had had a similar difficulty as a child. Below I share the concrete documents that came out of his program. Turan's case shows in particular how deeply a physiological problem can affect social life — a proper intervention can change not just the 'dry night' statistic, but the child's whole social world.

Therapeutic Assessment Form

Presenting problemWetting the bed almost every night, avoiding social activities

Treatment goalStrengthen the brain-body connection with the bell-and-pad system, reduce shame

In this form, the 'core belief' column requires special attention in enuresis cases — because children often don't express this thought openly, they simply show it through social avoidance behavior. Turan's refusal of birthday parties, if not asked about directly, could easily be misread as simple 'shyness.'

Sample Session Protocol

Assessment and pediatric confirmation1Organic cause ruled out, functional enuresis confirmed

Note in Turan's example that returning to social activities (attending birthday parties) was tracked as one of the final assessment criteria — this is an important indicator of how clinical outcomes (number of dry nights) translate not just into statistics, but into the child's real quality of life.

Therapist–Patient Dialogue

T: Turan, did you know your dad had a similar difficulty at your age?

Turan: Really? I had no idea.

T: Yes, and it fully resolved as he grew up too. This is something that runs in the family, it's not your fault.

Turan: (relaxes) So I'm not strange?

T: Not at all, you're not doing anything wrong — your body just needs time.

Turan: (smiles) It's good to know that.

Sample Homework

The family was advised to gradually reduce fluid intake after 6 PM and to make Turan's bathroom visit before bed a consistent routine. After the bell-and-pad system was applied, they were asked to note the dry/wet outcome each night without judgment. Almost no change was seen in the first two weeks, but from the third week on, dry nights gradually began to increase — this steady, cumulative progress pattern is typical of the bell-and-pad system.

Assessment Tool: The Bell-and-Pad System

About the test: The bell-and-pad system, in use since the 1930s, is a classic behavior-therapy-based enuresis treatment tool that detects the first drop of moisture through a sensor pad and wakes the child with a sound signal.

Why it is administered: To build a conditioned link between bladder fullness and the waking response, as the method with the highest long-term success rate.

Who can administer it: A clinical psychologist, with the pediatrician's recommendation and supervision.

Materials used: A bell-and-pad device, an outcome tracking chart.

The bell-and-pad system's advantage over medication lies in its durability — with medication, the symptom often returns once treatment stops, whereas because the bell-and-pad physically rebuilds the brain-body connection, results usually remain more stable.

Recommendations for Parents

🚫 Never shame the child

Shame only increases stress and can worsen the situation — this isn't the child's choice.

🩺 See a pediatrician first

Ruling out organic causes is the first condition of every program.

🎉 Note small progress

Small steps (one dry night) before full success should also be praised.

Risk Assessment and Crisis Protocol

⚠️ Signs of medical urgency and emotional crisis

  • Blood in urine/stool, severe pain, fever — requires immediate pediatric examination;
  • Severe bullying at school over enuresis/encopresis, related expressions of hopelessness;
  • Sudden onset of the symptom (in a child who previously had full control) — may be an indicator of trauma/stress.
  1. If medical signs (blood, pain, fever) are reported, psychological work is immediately paused and a pediatric referral is made.
  2. When hopeless language related to bullying is heard, ask the risk question directly and contact the school.
  3. In cases of sudden onset, investigate stress factors in the family/environment over recent weeks (loss, separation, abuse).
  4. Work with the parent to reduce shame — a punitive reaction always worsens the symptom.
