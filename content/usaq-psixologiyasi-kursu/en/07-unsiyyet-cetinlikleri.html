Topic 07 · Language and Communication

Communication Difficulties

Working with receptive, expressive, and pragmatic language difficulties — a structured 8-session program.

1

Scientific Definition: The Clinical Foundations of Communication Disorders

Clinical definition: According to the DSM-5-TR, Language Disorder involves persistent difficulties at the vocabulary, sentence-construction, or discourse level that are inconsistent with age. This can appear in three domains: receptive (understanding heard/read language), expressive (expressing thought in words), and pragmatic (using language appropriate to social context — which has its own separate category, Social Communication Disorder). In addition, Speech Sound Disorder (articulation) and Childhood-Onset Fluency Disorder (stuttering) are separate diagnostic categories.
In simple terms: Think of communication as a 'postal system': writing a letter (expression), reading a letter (understanding), and sending the letter to the right address in the right form (social use). Difficulty can occur at any of these three stages, and each requires different support.

An important distinction: delay — language develops in the same sequence, but slowly; disorder — language develops in an atypical pattern. Both require professional support, but the prognosis can differ.

When explaining this distinction to parents, I always emphasize: in a delay, the child is simply progressing on the same tracks with a 'late-running train,' and often catches up to peers with early support. In a disorder, the tracks themselves are different, so intervention is longer-term and more specific. Making this distinction correctly always requires a certified speech-language pathologist's assessment — parent observation is valuable, but not sufficient on its own.

2

Classification of Difficulties

👂

Receptive (Understanding)

Not understanding multi-step instructions, failing to answer questions correctly, difficulty following a story.

💬

Expressive (Expression)

Limited vocabulary, short/grammatically simple sentences, difficulty organizing thought.

🤝

Pragmatic (Social use)

Not taking turns in conversation, not staying on topic, not choosing a tone of language appropriate to context.

Assessing these three domains separately is critical, because intervention looks completely different for each. For receptive difficulty, visual support and simplified language are the main tools; for expressive difficulty, modeling and expansion take priority; for pragmatic difficulty, work centers on role-play and social scenarios.

3

Multidisciplinary Perspective (5 specialist views)

🧠 Psychological perspective

Long-term expressive difficulty can affect self-esteem and social connections in the preschool years — early support prevents this.

✋ Occupational therapist perspective

Oral-motor coordination (chewing, swallowing) affects the formation of speech sounds, especially in articulation difficulties.

🌊 Sensory Integration perspective

Auditory processing sensitivity can cause a child to 'hear but not understand' what's being said.

🗣️ Speech-language perspective

Assessing and treating language disorders is the speech-language pathologist's core specialty — all three domains (receptive, expressive, pragmatic) are assessed separately.

🏫 Psychopedagogical perspective

Visual support (pictures, signs) in the classroom environment helps children with language difficulty follow academic material.

The reason I apply these five perspectives together is simple — language difficulty is rarely confined to a single, pure domain. An occupational therapist's oral-motor assessment supports the speech-language pathologist's articulation work, while a sensory specialist's auditory-processing observations shed light on the question of 'why does the child hear, but not respond.'

4

Focus by Age Group

👶 1-3 years

  • First words, gesture use, response to simple instructions
  • No words by 18 months is a notable warning sign

🎨 3-6 years

  • Sentence construction, asking questions, following a story
  • Sound articulation (sounds like r, s, sh) is still forming

📚 6-12 years

  • Complex grammar, language skills tied to reading and writing
  • Pragmatic subtleties in conversation (understanding humor, irony)

Considering the age factor is decisive for the early-intervention decision. Having no words by 18 months isn't a situation to 'wait and see' — research shows that children who receive early support at this age progress faster than those who receive intervention later.

5

Core Model: The Communication Development Ladder

Communication development proceeds in sequential steps — each step builds on the one before it.

👋

1. Gesture/Sound

Pointing, waving, using sound to draw attention

🔤

2. Single Word

Words expressing a concrete need ('water', 'mom')

📖

3. Sentence/Conversation

Fully expressing a thought, engaging in reciprocal dialogue

💡 Important note: Intervention should always target one step above the child's current step — too big a leap reduces motivation.

In practice, I map this ladder individually for every child — because two children of the same age can be on different steps. Choosing the wrong step (e.g., directly demanding a complex sentence from a child at the single-word level) creates frustration and can, in fact, reduce communication attempts.

Clinical Case: Aydin's Story

When working on language difficulties, I always remind parents that what matters as much as early intervention is patience. Aydin was a 4-year-old boy; his parents reported that he understood words, but had great difficulty expressing himself, communicating mainly with single words or gestures. Speech-language assessment showed receptive language was age-appropriate, while expressive language showed a significant delay. Below I share the concrete documents that came out of his program. This 'understands well, but speaks little' profile is quite common in speech-language practice and can show very rapid progress with the right intervention.

Therapeutic Assessment Form

When filling out this form, I direct most of my attention to the 'perpetuating factors' column — in Aydin's case, the family's immediate understanding of his gesture was well-intentioned, but it was a factor reducing the need for verbal expression.

Sample Session Protocol

StageSessionNote
Language assessment1Receptive normal, a 12-month expressive delay identified
Parent psychoeducation2Wait-time and expansion techniques taught

The point to note in this chart is the sequence — parent psychoeducation must always come before expressive intervention, because without a change in the family's daily language behavior, progress made in clinical sessions doesn't transfer home.

Therapist–Patient Dialogue

T: Aydin, what's this? (shows a ball)

Aydin: Ball.

T: Yes, a ball! A red ball. Would you like to throw the ball?

Aydin: (throws the ball) Ball goes!

T: Yes, the ball goes far! You said that beautifully.

Aydin: (smiles, throws it again) Ball goes!

Sample Homework

Parents were advised that instead of immediately giving Aydin what he wanted with a gesture, they should ask 'what do you want?' and wait 5 seconds, accepting even a single-word answer as satisfactory. They were asked to note how many new words Aydin used over the course of a week. This task felt hard for parents at first — they worried the child would become distressed — but after a few days they saw that Aydin himself was trying to find the word during the waiting moment.

Assessment Tool: CELF (Clinical Evaluation of Language Fundamentals)

About the test: CELF, developed by Eleanor Semel and colleagues, is an internationally recognized standardized language assessment tool that evaluates receptive and expressive language with separate subtests.

Why it is administered: To objectively determine in which domain (understanding, expression, or both) and to what degree the language difficulty exists.

Who can administer it: A certified speech-language pathologist.

Materials used: CELF stimulus booklet, response sheets, picture cards, record protocol.

CELF's most valuable feature is that it shows receptive and expressive scores separately — this reveals exactly where intervention should focus in children with a profile like Aydin's ('understands, but speaks little'), instead of a general 'language delay' label.

Recommendations for Parents

⏳ Wait for a response

After asking a question, wait at least 5-7 seconds — give the child time to gather their thoughts.

🔁 Expand, don't correct

If the child says 'dog run,' expand by repeating 'yes, the dog is running!' — don't correct directly.

📚 Read books together

Picture books are the most effective daily tool for expanding vocabulary.

Risk Assessment and Crisis Protocol

⚠️ Risk hidden behind communication difficulty

Children who cannot speak or have limited language skills are deprived of the ability to report abuse — this requires the therapist to show additional sensitivity:

  • An unusual behavioral change (regression, aggression, withdrawal) without explanation;
  • A strong, unexplained fear reaction toward a specific person/place;
  • Concerning repetitive signs in body language or gestures.
  1. Create an opportunity to ask the child simple questions like 'are you safe, did someone hurt you' through Alternative Communication (AAC, PECS, picture cards).
  2. Map the timeline of the behavioral change together with the parent — does it coincide with a specific event?
  3. If suspicion appears grounded, immediately begin the reporting procedure without using the communication limitation as an excuse — 'cannot speak' never means 'is safe.'
  4. Conduct a joint assessment with a multidisciplinary team (speech-language pathologist + psychologist + social worker).

📌 Topic Summary

  • Communication difficulties can occur in receptive, expressive, and pragmatic domains, each requiring different support.
  • Communication development proceeds in sequential steps — intervention should target one step above the current one.
  • Early intervention supports both language and social-emotional development.
  • The parent's daily language behavior (waiting, expanding) increases the program's effectiveness.
