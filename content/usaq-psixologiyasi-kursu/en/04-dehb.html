Topic 04 · ADHD

Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD)

A comprehensive 24-session program and a condensed 15-session program — a complete support model from theory to the classroom.

1

Scientific Definition: The Clinical Foundations of ADHD

Clinical definition: According to the DSM-5-TR, Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) is a persistent pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity that is inconsistent with developmental level and interferes with functioning. Symptoms must begin before age 12, be observed in at least 2 different settings (e.g., home and school), and persist for more than 6 months. There are three presentations: predominantly inattentive, predominantly hyperactive-impulsive, and combined type. Neurobiologically, ADHD is linked to differences in the regulation of executive functions in the prefrontal cortex, involving dopamine and norepinephrine transporters.
In simple terms: An ADHD brain can be compared to a browser with 20 tabs open at once — everything is active, but choosing "which one to focus on" becomes difficult. Or like a car with a slightly weak "braking system" — there's plenty of speed, but the ability to stop on time or slow down for a turn needs extra practice.

ADHD is not "misbehavior" or "a parenting mistake" — it's a brain-based difference. With the right structure, these children have enormous potential for creativity, energy, and spontaneity.

2

Clinical Classification of Symptoms

🌫️

Inattention

Not attending to details, failing to finish tasks, forgetfulness, difficulty with organization, easily distracted attention.

E.g.: starts homework but switches to something else before finishing.

⚡

Hyperactivity

Unable to stay seated, excessive talking, a constant need to be in motion, difficulty with quiet play.

E.g.: constantly fidgeting in the chair during class.

🎯

Impulsivity

Unable to wait for a turn, blurting out an answer before the question is finished, interrupting others, acting without thinking.

E.g.: butting into a game without waiting for their turn.

3

Multidisciplinary Perspective (5 specialist views)

🧠 Psychological perspective

ADHD frequently co-occurs (comorbid) with anxiety or a behavior disorder — this must always be considered during assessment.

✋ Occupational therapist perspective

Fine motor skills (handwriting) and self-management strategies (taking breaks, incorporating movement) significantly improve daily functioning.

🌊 Sensory Integration perspective

In some children, hyperactivity is actually "sensory seeking" — an excessive need for movement may be an attempt to regulate the body.

🗣️ Speech-language / Communication perspective

Impulsive responding and difficulty taking turns in conversation may be linked to pragmatic language skills.

🏫 Psychopedagogical perspective

The structure of the classroom environment (seating, breaking tasks into parts) directly affects symptom severity.

4

Presentation by Age Group

👶 Preschool (4-6 years)

  • The hyperactive-impulsive form is usually more visible
  • Frequent switching of activity during play
  • Early intervention (parent training) is the priority

📚 Primary school (6-11 years)

  • Decline in academic performance draws attention
  • Difficulty in social relationships (impulsivity affects friendships)
  • Structure, visual support, and a reward system are effective

🎓 Adolescence (12+ years)

  • Hyperactivity can turn into inner restlessness
  • Organization and time management become the main problem area
  • Focus shifts increasingly to self-awareness and self-management strategies

Core Model: Executive Functions

ADHD isn't really about attention — it's about the brain's "chief manager" (the prefrontal cortex) working less efficiently. Four core executive functions are the target of intervention:

🛑 Inhibition (Impulse Control)

The ability to pause and think instead of reacting immediately.

💾 Working Memory

The ability to hold instructions or information in mind while carrying out a task.

🗂️ Planning/Organization

The ability to break a task into steps and determine the sequence.

🌡️ Emotional Regulation

The ability to manage frustration and excitement while continuing purposefully toward a goal.

💡 Important note: Each of these 4 functions is a "muscle" that can be trained separately — ADHD programs focus precisely on strengthening these, not just on telling the child to "pay attention."

Clinical Case: Rufat's Story

When building an ADHD program, I always clarify one thing up front — this isn't about "calming the child down," it's about teaching him to manage his own brain. Rufat was an 8-year-old boy in second grade; his teacher reported that he constantly got up from his seat during class, blurted out answers before questions were finished, and never fully completed his homework. Assessment with the Conners-3 scale confirmed combined-type ADHD. Below I share the concrete documents that came out of his program.

Sample Homework

Parents were advised to break Rufat's homework time into 10-minute segments, giving a 2-minute movement break after each segment, using a timer. They were asked to note over the course of a week how many segments he could complete without interruption.

Assessment Tool: The Conners-3 Rating Scale

About the test: The Conners-3, developed by Keith Conners, is an internationally standard scale consisting of parent, teacher, and self-report forms, assessing ADHD symptoms from multiple sources.

Why it is administered: To objectively measure the frequency and severity of ADHD symptoms and in which settings (home, school) they appear most, and to support the treatment plan.

Who can administer it: A clinical psychologist or a specialist trained in ADHD assessment.

Materials used: Conners-3 parent/teacher/self-report forms, a scoring program or chart.

Recommendations for Parents

⏱️ Give short, clear instructions

One task at a time — use simple, concrete sentences instead of multi-step commands.

🏃 Plan movement breaks

Short movement breaks between activities requiring extended sitting improve performance.

🎯 Praise the effort, not the outcome

"You tried really hard" builds more lasting motivation than "you're smart."

Risk Assessment and Crisis Protocol

⚠️ Safety and medical collaboration in ADHD

  • When high impulsivity puts the child at physical risk (running into traffic, jumping from heights);
  • Signs of heart palpitations, chest pain, or loss of consciousness in children taking stimulant medication;
  • Comorbid Oppositional Defiant Disorder or Conduct Disorder symptoms escalating into aggression.
  1. When a high physical-risk behavior is identified, build a concrete environmental safety plan (supervision, barriers) together with the parent.
  2. If cardiovascular symptoms related to medication are reported, immediately recommend contacting the prescribing physician — this is beyond the scope of psychological intervention and requires urgent medical assessment.
  3. If escalation to aggression is observed, refer in parallel to the crisis protocol in the Working with Aggressive Children program.
  4. Encourage setting up a school-related safety plan (recess supervision, transition support).

📌 Topic Summary

  • ADHD is a neurobiologically based disorder presenting in three forms (inattentive, hyperactive-impulsive, combined).
  • Intervention focuses on four executive functions (inhibition, working memory, planning, emotional regulation).
  • The 15-session model suits mild-to-moderate cases, the 24-session model suits moderate-to-severe cases.
  • Parent-school collaboration is decisive for the durability of outcomes.
