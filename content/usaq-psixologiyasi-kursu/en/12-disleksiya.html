A full program with 10 workbooks — a structured path from phonological processing to fluent reading.

1

Scientific Definition: The Clinical Foundations of Dyslexia

Clinical definition: In the DSM-5-TR, Dyslexia is classified as a subtype of the Specific Learning Disorder category — characterized by accurate and/or fluent word recognition difficulty, weak letter-sound decoding, and weak spelling skills. Neurologically, dyslexia is linked to different activation patterns in the brain's left-hemisphere language-processing networks (particularly the temporo-parietal and occipito-temporal regions related to phonological processing). This is independent of intellectual ability — most children with dyslexia have normal or above-normal IQ.
In simple terms: A dyslexic brain does the process of 'connecting' letters with sounds in a different, more difficult way — it's like decoding a new code every time. This isn't about 'laziness' or 'not reading enough,' it's about how the brain processes language.

Dyslexia shows a hereditary pattern, and with early, correct intervention, most children can develop effective reading strategies — it isn't 'cured,' it's 'compensated for.'

Working in this field over the years, the tragedy I encounter most is the damage that unrecognized dyslexia does to a child's self-esteem. The reading difficulty itself, with the right method, can improve relatively quickly; but the belief 'I'm stupid,' once formed, can persist for years. That's why the parallel goal of every program, alongside academic skill, is for the child to understand and accept how their own brain works.

2

Classification of Symptoms

🔤

Phonological Processing

Difficulty distinguishing sounds, recognizing rhyme, mastering letter-sound correspondence.

📖

Reading Fluency

Slow, effortful reading, guessing words, losing the line, difficulty comprehending what's read.

✍️

Spelling (Orthography)

Frequent letter-sequence errors, phonetic spelling (writing as it sounds).

3

Multidisciplinary Perspective (5 specialist views)

🧠 Psychological perspective

Persistent academic failure seriously affects self-esteem — emotional support is as important as academic intervention.

✋ Occupational therapist perspective

Hand-eye coordination difficulties during writing can co-occur with dysgraphia and require separate support.

🌊 Sensory Integration perspective

Visual processing differences (difficulty tracking a line) can add further difficulty to the reading process.

🗣️ Speech-language perspective

Phonological awareness (hearing, distinguishing, blending sounds) is the main target of speech-language intervention.

🏫 Psychopedagogical perspective

Structured, sequential, multisensory teaching methods (Orton-Gillingham type) give the most effective results.

These five perspectives working together is especially important, because dyslexia is rarely 'pure' — when the writing difficulty noted by the occupational therapist combines with the visual tracking problem seen by the sensory specialist, a speech-language approach alone isn't enough. When the team works together, the child's full picture becomes visible.

4

Focus by Age Group

👶 4-6 years

  • Early signs: difficulty with rhyme, trouble recalling letter names
  • Phonological awareness games are the basis for early intervention

🎨 6-9 years

  • A formal diagnosis is usually made in this age range
  • Systematic phonics and multisensory techniques are applied

📚 9+ years

  • Compensation strategies (audiobooks, text-to-speech programs) become a priority
  • Support for self-esteem and academic motivation increases significantly
5

Core Model: The Orton-Gillingham Approach

Orton-Gillingham is the most researched structured teaching method for dyslexia, based on multisensory (sight+hearing+touch+movement) principles.

👁️

Sight

Seeing the letter and recognizing its visual form

👂

Hearing

Hearing the letter's sound and pronouncing it

✋

Touch/Movement

'Writing' the letter in sand, in the air, or on a textured surface

💡 Important note: Using these three channels simultaneously helps the letter-sound connection form more strongly in memory.

In practice, I never rush this triad — I make sure all three channels are used for every new letter, because if one channel is skipped (e.g., only seeing and hearing, skipping touch), the memory trace remains weak and the child may forget the letter after a few days. This is the most common mistake made by hurried specialists.

Clinical Case: Aysu's Story

When working with dyslexia, I always begin my first words to the child with the same sentence: 'your brain is different, not deficient.' Aysu was an 8-year-old girl in second grade; while her classmates already read fluently, she was still sounding out letters one by one. Her teacher called her 'inattentive,' and her parents thought she 'worked too little.' The RAN (Rapid Automatized Naming) test came out well below the norm, and phonological awareness was weak — these showed that Aysu's problem wasn't 'laziness,' but a neurologically based phonological processing difference. Below I share the concrete documents that came out of her program. Aysu's story is typical — children with dyslexia are often first labeled 'inattentive' or 'lazy,' while a correct assessment opens up a completely different, more accurate, and more hopeful picture.

In this form, the 'core belief' column required special attention in Aysu's program — academic techniques alone weren't enough, because the belief 'I'm stupid' had already formed. That's why small, concrete successes were specially highlighted at every session, to gradually shake this belief.

The path Aysu took across the 10 workbooks actually reflects the common logic of all dyslexia programs: first recognizing sounds, then connecting them to letters, then blending them, then speeding up, and finally having capacity left over to focus on meaning. Each step builds on the previous one, none is skipped.

Therapist–Patient Dialogue

T: Aysu, we'll learn this letter in three different ways — you'll see it, hear it, feel it.

Aysu: I can never remember letters, this probably won't work either.

T: This isn't about your intelligence — your brain just learns in a different way, this method is exactly for brains like yours.

Aysu: (hesitantly) Okay, I'll try.

T: (shows the letter in sand) Now you draw it yourself and say its sound.

Aysu: (writes, says it out loud) That was easier than before!

Sample Homework

Parents were asked to do a 10-minute repeated reading practice with Aysu every evening — having her read the same short text 3 times, noting the time each time, and showing Aysu the result so she could see her own progress in numbers. It was emphasized that the goal isn't perfect reading, but beating her own previous self. This small shift — comparing against her own past result instead of her classmates — showed a noticeable effect on Aysu's academic self-esteem within a few weeks.

Assessment Tool: The RAN (Rapid Automatized Naming) Test

About the test: RAN, developed by Martha Denckla and Rita Rudel in 1976, is a standardized test measuring how quickly a child can name familiar visual stimuli (colors, numbers, letters).

Why it is administered: RAN speed is one of the strongest early predictors of future reading fluency — a separate risk indicator, independent of phonological awareness.

Who can administer it: A clinical/school psychologist or a specialist specialized in dyslexia.

Materials used: RAN stimulus cards (color, number, letter sequences), a stopwatch, a record protocol.

The RAN result is one of the most convincing documents I show parents — because it directly refutes the 'inattentiveness' interpretation: the child recognizes the colors, they simply show a delay in the process of naming them QUICKLY, which is a neurological indicator, not a behavioral choice.

Recommendations for Parents

🎧 Use audiobooks

This is a great alternative so reading difficulty doesn't block access to content.

⏱️ Reduce time pressure

Pressure related to reading speed increases anxiety and worsens performance.

💪 Highlight strengths

Children with dyslexia are often strong in creativity, problem-solving, and verbal expression — note this.

Risk Assessment and Crisis Protocol

⚠️ Emotional crisis related to academic failure

  • Persistent bullying over dyslexia, being labeled 'stupid' — a sharp decline in self-respect;
  • Outright refusal to go to school, panic reactions related to books/writing;
  • Expressions of hopelessness ('I'll never be able to read, it's pointless') — risk of transition into depression.
  1. Ask directly about emotional state at every session, separate from academic progress.
  2. If bullying is reported, contact school leadership and demand concrete action.
  3. When hopeless language is encountered, ask the risk question directly and share it with the parent.
  4. Establish formal contact with the school for academic accommodations (extra time, alternative assessment) — this is a key factor reducing the emotional burden.

📌 Topic Summary

  • Dyslexia is a neurologically based phonological processing difficulty, independent of intelligence.
  • Orton-Gillingham-type multisensory methods are the most effective evidence-based approach.
  • Early intervention (ages 4-6) significantly improves long-term outcomes.
  • Emotional support and protecting self-esteem are as important as academic intervention.
