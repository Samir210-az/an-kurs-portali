Topic 14 · Social Anxiety and Speech

Mutism (Selective Mutism)

12 full interactive sessions plus an intensification guide for severe cases — gradual, pressure-free work with anxiety-based mutism.

1

Scientific Definition: The Clinical Foundations of Selective Mutism

Clinical definition: In the DSM-5-TR, Selective Mutism falls under the anxiety disorders category — characterized by a child's persistent failure to speak in specific social situations where speaking is expected (e.g., at school), while speaking normally in other situations (e.g., at home). This must persist more than 1 month and interfere with academic/social functioning. The core mechanism isn't a language difficulty, but acute social anxiety and performance fear — the child doesn't 'refuse' to speak, they 'can't,' because anxiety 'locks' the speech system.
In simple terms: Imagine you know a lot, but feel your tongue 'freeze' in front of a large audience. For a child with selective mutism, this feeling occurs in almost every social situation — it isn't stubbornness, it's an acute 'freeze' response.

The most significant mistake is forcing the child to speak. This increases anxiety, and instead of expecting a 'leap,' progress must happen through very small, gradual steps.

2

Classification of Presentations

🏠

Normal Speech at Home

Full, comfortable speech with family members, in a familiar environment.

🏫

Silence at School/in Public

Complete silence in front of the teacher, classmates, sometimes with nonverbal communication (nodding) preserved.

😰

Physical Anxiety Signs

Freezing when speech is expected, flushing, avoiding eye contact, bodily tension.

3

Multidisciplinary Perspective (5 specialist views)

🧠 Psychological perspective

Selective mutism is often related to social anxiety disorder — cognitive-behavioral therapy is the main treatment method.

✋ Occupational therapist perspective

Sensory-motor activities that reduce bodily tension can provide the ease needed for speech.

🌊 Sensory Integration perspective

High sensory sensitivity (noise, crowds) can intensify anxiety and worsen the mutism.

🗣️ Speech-language perspective

The speech-language pathologist plays a key role in building a gradual speech hierarchy and in the 'stimulus fading' technique.

🏫 Psychopedagogical perspective

A teacher's pressure-free, patiently waiting stance (not forcing speech) protects the child's sense of safety.

4

Focus by Age Group

👶 3-5 years

  • First signs can appear connected to starting kindergarten
  • Early intervention (gradual exposure) is very effective

🎨 5-9 years

  • Impact on academic assessment can appear in the school setting
  • Stimulus fading (gradual transition from familiar to new person) is the core technique

📚 9+ years

  • Risk of social isolation increases, making friendships harder
  • Self-awareness and the child's active involvement in their own process is important

Core Model: The Speech Bridge (Stimulus Fading)

Stimulus fading is an evidence-based behavioral technique that provides a gradual transition from a person/setting the child speaks comfortably with to a new person/setting.

👪

1. With a Familiar Person

Starting in the room with the parent they speak comfortably with

🚪

2. New Person Entry

A new person gradually enters the room, from a distance

🗣️

3. Direct Communication

Direct, gradually expanding interaction with the new person

Clinical Case: Shabnam's Story

When working with selective mutism, I emphasize to the family from the first minute: this isn't stubbornness, it's a 'freezing' of the speech system. Shabnam was a 6-year-old girl; although she was a very talkative, cheerful child at home, a year after starting kindergarten she still hadn't said a single word to her caregiver. Her parents first considered this 'shyness' and waited, then began to think 'she's doing it on purpose to embarrass us.' Anxiety mapping showed that Shabnam's speaking difficulty was entirely related to social anxiety — there was no problem with her language ability. Below I share the concrete documents that came out of her program.

Sample Homework

Parents were advised to never force Shabnam with 'say it,' and instead to take every nonverbal attempt (nodding, pointing) just as seriously as a verbal response, and to praise it. Starting from the lowest-scoring situations on the anxiety map, brief, gradual encounters with new people were planned.

Assessment Tool: The Selective Mutism Questionnaire (SMQ)

About the test: The SMQ (Selective Mutism Questionnaire) is a standardized questionnaire that systematically assesses the frequency of a child's speech across various social settings and people, based on parent/teacher report.

Why it is administered: To precisely map in which contexts speech is restricted and to determine the starting point of the intervention hierarchy.

Who can administer it: A clinical psychologist or a specialist trained in anxiety disorders.

Materials used: SMQ parent/teacher forms, an anxiety scale card.

How to Extend the Program in Severe Cases: A Practical Guide

12 sessions is enough for mild-to-moderate cases. For more severe, long-standing selective mutism, the same 12 techniques are needed, just at a slower pace, with more steps. Below I show concrete examples of how I do this.

Rushing in selective mutism causes more harm than in other areas — because once a child feels 'forced,' rebuilding trust is harder than the original situation. So when I see a 'severe case,' I never speed up the same 12 steps, instead I break each step down even further.

1. When are 12 sessions not enough?

SignWhat it means
Complete silence lasting more than 1 yearThe habit has deepened, more repetition is needed for each step
Silence across several settings (school + public places)The generalization stage needs to be significantly expanded
Reluctance even in nonverbal communicationAdditional trust-building time is needed before session 3
A previous 'forcing' experience occurredA longer psychoeducation phase is needed to restore trust

2. How to extend — a concrete example (Session 4, the whisper stage)

For the clearest example, let's take session 4 — whispering through the parent. In a mild case, this happens in one or two sessions. In a severe case, I break this into 4 sessions:

SessionFocusConcrete variable
4aWhispering to the parent, therapist not in the roomThe therapist briefly steps out of the room, then returns
4bWhispering to the parent, therapist sits at a distanceThe therapist is in the room, but distant, not participating verbally
4cWhispering to the parent, therapist sits nearbyThe distance gradually decreases
4dWhispering to the parent, the answer is relayed to the therapistA bridge to the next session (direct whispering)

None of these 4 steps is a new technique — all are the same 'whispering through the parent' principle, just the therapist's physical distance gradually changes. This rule can be applied to all 12 techniques.

3. In what order to expand

The same rule applies here too: first repetition, then depth, and finally breadth.

🔁

Repetition

Repeat the same step in the same setting several times, until fully comfortable. Don't rush.

🔍

Depth

Then make the step a bit more difficult — from whisper to moderate volume, from a single word to a sentence.

🌍

Breadth

Finally test with a different person, place — this is generalization.

The most common mistake in selective mutism is rushing to breadth (testing with a new person, while the child is still at the repetition stage with just one person). This causes regression. First it needs to be fully consolidated with one person, in one place.

Full example: How 12 sessions become 26

Core sessionIn a severe caseHow it's broken down

This chart is a framework, not a strict rule — I ask the three questions above (when, how, in what order) again for each child. For Shabnam herself, for example, the whisper stage took only 2 sessions, but meeting a new person required 3 — every child has their own pace.

Recommendations for Parents

🚫 Don't force speech

The demand 'say it' increases anxiety and can set the process back.

🎉 Praise nonverbal attempts too

Nodding, pointing are also communication attempts and deserve praise.

🤝 Stay in close contact with the school

A teacher's pressure-free, patient stance is essential for the process's success.

11

Risk Assessment and Crisis Protocol

⚠️ The difference between selective mutism and trauma-based silence

  • Sudden onset of silence (in a previously freely speaking child) — unlike Selective Mutism, this may be a trauma indicator;
  • Silence restricted to a specific person/place, physical tension signs in contact with that person;
  • A parallel increase in hiding, shrinking behaviors in body language.
  1. Clarify the onset timeline — was it gradual (consistent with Selective Mutism) or sudden (trauma suspected)?
  2. If tension in contact with a specific person is identified, conduct additional, careful investigation about that person.
  3. Create an opportunity to ask the child a safety question through alternative communication (writing, gesture, drawing).
  4. If suspicion appears grounded, don't accept the mutism as 'just shyness' and begin the reporting procedure.

📌 Topic Summary

  • Selective mutism is anxiety-based, not stubbornness or manipulation.
  • Stimulus fading — a gradual transition from familiar to new — is the main evidence-based technique.
  • Forcing speech backfires — a gradual, pressure-free approach is required.
  • Home-school collaboration and patience are decisive for the program's success.
