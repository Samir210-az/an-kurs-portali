Topic 02 · Assessment Tools

Psychological Assessment Tools

Denver II, AGTE, CAT, IQ tests, Gesell, Bender-Gestalt, Bourdon, D2, and the Autism screening test — the scientific basis, administration procedure, and interpretation logic of each.

1

Scientific Definition: Foundations of Psychometric Assessment

Clinical definition: A standardized psychological test is a measurement instrument that has been validated on a large normative sample, with demonstrated reliability and validity, and is administered the same way every time. These tests compare a child's result against the normative base of thousands of same-age peers, showing which percentile their development falls into. Tests fall into three main categories: screening tools (quick, broad screening), diagnostic tools (deep, specific), and projective tools (revealing subconscious content through symbolic tasks).
In simple terms: A standardized test is like weighing a child's "growth line" on a scale. Just as a doctor compares height and weight against the normal chart for thousands of children, we measure attention, language, movement, and emotional development with the same logic — the difference is that the chart is built on skills, not numbers.

No test alone "makes a diagnosis" — every result must be interpreted together with clinical observation, history, and other tests.

Over years of conducting assessments, I want to clear up a misconception I often encounter: parents sometimes ask "which test is better," but the question is framed wrong — tools don't replace each other, they complement each other. Denver II is excellent for a quick screen, but WISC-V is needed for a deep cognitive profile; CAT opens up the emotional world but doesn't measure attention performance. That's why at our center I always start by asking "which question needs answering," and only then choose the matching tool — never the other way around.

2

Classification of Tools

📈

Screening tools

Denver II, the Gesell scale — assess broad developmental domains through quick screening, answering the question "is further evaluation needed?"

🧩

Diagnostic/cognitive tools

IQ tests (WISC, Stanford-Binet), Bender-Gestalt, Bourdon and D2 — measure specific cognitive domains (reasoning, attention, visual-motor coordination) in depth.

🎭

Projective/specific tools

The CAT (Children's Apperception Test) measures the emotional world, while autism assessment tests (M-CHAT, CARS) specifically measure social-communicative development.

3

Multidisciplinary Perspective

🧠 Psychological perspective

Every test has its own "margin of error" — a result should be read not as a single number, but as a confidence interval.

✋ Occupational therapy / Sensory integration perspective

Visual-motor tests like Bender-Gestalt provide additional, highly valuable information about hand-eye coordination and graphomotor maturity.

🗣️ Speech-language / Communication perspective

The language section of the Denver II and the storytelling ability in the CAT reveal the level of expressive language development.

🏫 Psychopedagogical perspective

The D2 and Bourdon attention tests help predict in advance a child's potential for sustaining attention on school tasks.

4

Suitable Tools by Age Group

👶 0–6 years

  • Denver II — developmental screening for ages 0-6 (4 domains: motor, language, social, fine motor)
  • Gesell developmental schedule — detailed norm chart for early ages
  • AGTE — general developmental assessment test

🎨 5–10 years

  • CAT — projective emotional test for ages 3-10
  • Bender-Gestalt — visual-motor maturity from age 5 onward
  • M-CHAT / CARS — screening and rating scale for autism spectrum

📚 6–16 years

  • IQ tests (WISC-V, Stanford-Binet) — cognitive profile
  • Bourdon and D2 attention tests — sustained attention and concentration
  • Additional questionnaires related to school performance

Clinical Case: Anar's Story

The example I'm sharing in this chapter is based on a profile that comes to our center often — attention and academic-readiness concerns in children at the pre-primary stage. Anar was a 7-year-old boy; after starting first grade, his teacher told his parents that he often left tasks unfinished and frequently got up from his seat during lessons. When the family came to us, the goal wasn't to rush to an answer for "is it ADHD?" but to map Anar's full cognitive and attention profile using multiple tools. In cases like this I never rely on a single test — each tool is one window, and the full picture only emerges when several come together. This approach matters especially because a decision based on a single observation can both steer the family in the wrong direction and risk placing an unnecessary label on the child.

Anar: Okay, but can it be quick?

Sample Homework

Anar's parents were asked to set up a 15-minute "quiet table time" with him every evening — screen-free, with a simple task (puzzle, coloring), sitting quietly beside him until the task was finished. They were asked to note over the course of a week how many minutes he could sustain without a break, so it could be compared with the D2 result at the next visit.

Assessment Tool: WISC-V (Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children)

About the test: The WISC-V is the fifth edition of David Wechsler's original work, and the most widely used cognitive assessment tool worldwide for children aged 6-16.

Why it is administered: To measure a child's overall cognitive index and five core indices (Verbal Comprehension, Visual-Spatial, Fluid Reasoning, Working Memory, Processing Speed) in order to identify strong and weak cognitive domains.

Who can administer it: Clinical or school psychologists formally certified in the WISC-V.

Materials used: Stimulus booklet, response sheets, block design cubes, stopwatch, record protocol.

Recommendations for Parents

😴 Bring your child well-rested

A tired or hungry child's test results may not reflect their real potential — good sleep and a meal on the day of testing matter.

🔢 Look at the profile, not a single number

What matters is the overall picture across all domains, not one score — a low result in one area can be balanced by strength in others.

🔁 Retesting is normal

Some tests are repeated after 6-12 months to track development — this doesn't mean "getting worse," it's a monitoring method.

Risk Assessment and Crisis Protocol

⚠️ When assessment tool results indicate risk

When results from standardized tools (Vanderbilt, the PHQ-9 Adolescent version, GAD-7, etc.) cross the clinical threshold, simply "noting it and moving on" is not enough:

  • A positive response to the suicide item on a depression/anxiety scale (e.g., PHQ-9 item 9);
  • A score at the acute clinical threshold on a trauma screening;
  • A serious discrepancy between parent and child reports (the child hides something, the parent doesn't notice).
  1. Any positive response to the suicide item automatically triggers a structured risk interview (see Module 15, cross-reference with the Divorce and Emotional Crisis section).
  2. Clinical-threshold results must be explained to the parent within the session, with the specific number and its meaning — not simply labeled "concerning."
  3. Results indicating high risk require re-assessment within 24-48 hours or a direct specialist referral.
  4. If a tool's result conflicts with clinical observation, always give priority to clinical judgment — no tool makes a diagnosis on its own.

📌 Topic Summary

  • Screening, diagnostic, and projective tools serve different purposes and complement one another.
  • Each tool has its own age range, administration time, and specific domain it measures.
  • Results are always interpreted relative to a normative base, accounting for measurement error.
  • A reliable clinical picture comes not from a single test, but from combining several tools.
