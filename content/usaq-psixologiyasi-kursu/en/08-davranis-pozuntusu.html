Behavior Disorder

A full 18-session program — structured work with Oppositional Defiant Disorder (ODD) and Conduct Disorder (CD).

1

Scientific Definition: The Clinical Foundations of Behavior Disorders

Clinical definition: According to the DSM-5-TR, Oppositional Defiant Disorder (ODD) is a pattern of angry/irritable mood, argumentative/defiant behavior, or vindictiveness lasting at least 6 months (e.g., frequent temper loss, arguing with adults, deliberately annoying others). Conduct Disorder (CD) is a more severe form — characterized by a persistent pattern of behavior that violates the basic rights of others or age-appropriate social norms (aggression, property destruction, deceit/theft, serious rule violations). Both are frequently linked to underlying regulation difficulty, low frustration tolerance, or learned behavioral patterns.
In simple terms: There's no such thing as a 'bad child' — a child with behavior problems usually simply doesn't yet have the 'tools' needed to manage their emotions. Their behavior is like a language — another way of saying 'I need help' or 'I can't manage myself.'

The difference between a behavior disorder and ordinary 'misbehavior' is frequency, severity, and persistence. Every child is defiant sometimes, but when this becomes a persistent pattern that interferes with daily functioning, it requires clinical attention.

Working in this field over the years, I never forget one principle — a child with behavior problems is actually the child who needs connection most, they've simply found the wrong way to ask for it. Punishment-based approaches may give short-term calm, but real change only happens when strengthening the relationship is combined with consistent structure.

Clinical Classification of Symptoms

😠

Angry/Irritable Mood

Frequently losing temper, getting angry, holding grudges against adults.

⚔️

Argumentative/Defiant Behavior

Persistent arguing with adults, refusing to comply with rules, deliberately annoying others.

🚨

Aggression/Norm Violation

(In severe forms) physical aggression, property damage, deceit, rule violations.

3

Multidisciplinary Perspective (5 specialist views)

🧠 Psychological perspective

Behavior problems are often the 'top layer' of underlying anxiety, trauma, or emotional regulation difficulty — the root must be addressed.

✋ Occupational therapist perspective

Some 'defiance' episodes may actually be an attempt to avoid sensory overload.

🌊 Sensory Integration perspective

Aggressive outbursts can sometimes be the result of a sensory 'fight-or-flight' reaction, not just 'bad intent.'

🗣️ Speech-language perspective

Weak pragmatic language skills can leave a child unable to verbally express a need, leading them to 'express it' through behavior instead.

🏫 Psychopedagogical perspective

Consistent, predictable classroom rules and a positive reinforcement system significantly reduce behavior problems.

Each of these five perspectives adds different information to the team — when the psychologist's iceberg model, the occupational therapist's sensory explanation, and the psychopedagogue's structural recommendation combine, we give the parent a fuller answer to 'why does he behave this way,' one that's more convincing than any single explanation.

4

Presentation by Age Group

👶 3-6 years

  • Tantrums, disobedience, hitting/biting
  • Early intervention (parent training) is the most effective period

🎨 6-12 years

  • Persistent arguing with adults, rule violations appear at school
  • Difficulty in social relationships (loss of friendships)

📚 12+ years

  • Risk of more serious normative violations (CD) increases
  • Peer influence and the identity-formation process play a significant role

The age factor is decisive for both prognosis and intervention strategy — parent training alone is often sufficient in the 3-6 age range, whereas in adolescence, the child's own active involvement in the process must go hand in hand with gradually reducing parental oversight.

5

Core Model: The Iceberg of Behavior

Visible behavior is always only the visible part of the iceberg — the underlying needs and emotions are the true target of intervention.

🌊 Visible (surface)

Anger outbursts, disobedience, aggression — these are the observed behaviors.

🧊 Invisible (depth)

Low frustration tolerance, lack of the ability to ask for help, past experiences, unmet needs.

💡 Example: The child throws a toy and screams (visible). The reason: they feel ashamed because they couldn't complete the task and don't know another way to express it (invisible). The intervention shouldn't be 'don't throw!' — it should be 'let's teach how to say you need help.'

When explaining this model to every family, I use concrete examples — an abstract explanation is often forgotten, but when shown on a specific event, like 'your child throwing things,' parents understand immediately and start applying it.

Clinical Case: Javid's Story

When working with behavior disorders, I always explain one thing to families: we need to stop thinking about the child being 'bad' and move to the question of 'which need is he still unable to put into words.' Javid was a 6-year-old boy who frequently had anger outbursts at kindergarten and at home, threw objects, and argued persistently with adults. Assessment with the Eyberg Child Behavior Inventory confirmed a pattern consistent with ODD criteria. Below I share the concrete documents that came out of his program. In cases like this, the biggest difficulty is often parents feeling guilty themselves — I always emphasize that the goal isn't to find blame, but for the family to learn new tools together as a team.

When filling out this form, the 'perpetuating factors' column requires the most work — inconsistent reactions usually stem from good intentions (not wanting to upset the child), but this exact thing becomes the mechanism that reinforces the behavior.

Therapist–Patient Dialogue

T: Javid, this puzzle is a bit hard, but you're ready for it.

Javid: (after several attempts) I can't do it, this is stupid!

T: I see you're frustrated. Let's take 3 deep breaths together, then try again.

Javid: (breathes) Okay, let me try once more.

T: You kept going despite the difficulty, that's a big skill.

Javid: (continues with a smile)

Sample Homework

Parents were advised to apply a daily 10-minute 'positive attention time' — accompanying Javid's chosen game with non-critical, descriptive praise. They were asked to note the frequency and duration of anger outbursts over the course of a week so that the effect of positive attention time could be objectively assessed at the next session. The family saw no difference for the first three to four days, which is completely normal — behavior change is a cumulative effect; the focus should be on consistency rather than expecting immediate results.

Assessment Tool: The Eyberg Child Behavior Inventory (ECBI)

About the test: The ECBI, developed by Sheila Eyberg, is a parent-rating scale for children ages 2-16 that measures the frequency of behavior problems and how problematic the parent considers these behaviors to be.

Why it is administered: To objectively measure the severity of behavior problems and create a baseline for pre/post-intervention comparison.

Who can administer it: A clinical psychologist or a specialist trained in behavior assessment.

Materials used: The ECBI parent questionnaire form, a scoring chart.

The ECBI's special value lies in measuring the 'intensity' (frequency) and 'problem' (how much the parent is bothered) scores separately — sometimes the same behavior is seen as 'manageable' in one family and 'unbearable' in another, and this difference determines treatment priority.

Recommendations for Parents

🧊 Stay calm yourself

Your own calmness when the child is angry is the strongest model teaching them how to calm down.

✅ Reward positive behavior with attention

Giving a lot of attention to bad behavior and little attention to good behavior actually reinforces the bad behavior.

📏 Set clear, consistent boundaries

When rules are unchanging and predictable, the child feels safer.

Risk Assessment and Crisis Protocol

⚠️ Signs of escalation in behavior disorders

  • Reaching for a weapon, a threat naming a specific target (plan+means+time);
  • Cruelty to animals, deliberate serious property damage — indicators of progression;
  • Aggression turning toward oneself, expressions of self-harm/suicide.
  1. Ask the risk question directly, in a calm tone: 'Have you had thoughts of hurting someone, or yourself?'
  2. If a real, imminent danger is identified, invite the parent into the session immediately, do not leave the child alone.
  3. If a plan+means+time is identified, refer immediately to emergency child psychiatric services.
  4. Discuss concretely with the parent about restricting access to weapons/harmful tools at home.
  5. Document every episode with the date, risk factors, and steps taken.

📌 Topic Summary

  • ODD and CD are behavior disorders on a spectrum, differentiated by frequency, severity, and persistence.
  • Visible behavior is always the 'tip of the iceberg' of underlying needs and emotions.
  • Consistent boundaries and positive reinforcement are the most effective long-term strategies.
  • A parent's calm, consistent reaction forms the foundation of behavior change.
