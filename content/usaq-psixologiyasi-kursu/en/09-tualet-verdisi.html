Toilet Training Program

A structured, pressure-free program from biological readiness to independent toilet use.

1

Scientific Definition: The Developmental Foundations of Toilet Learning

Clinical definition: Toilet training is a complex developmental process requiring the integration of voluntary control of the sphincter muscles, recognition of body signals, and the ability to translate these signals into socially appropriate behavior (going to the toilet). The American Academy of Pediatrics identifies indicators of biological readiness as: being able to hold urine for more than 2 hours, facial expressions/words signaling urination, lengthening dry periods, and showing interest in the toilet. Starting early (before physical readiness) can prolong the process and cause resistance. This isn't just a theoretical warning — in clinical practice, a significant portion of early-starting cases end up with a longer, more difficult process later.
In simple terms: Toilet training is like 'learning to walk' — every child becomes ready on their own timeline, and it can't be accelerated through force. Our job isn't to 'force,' it's to 'support when ready.' Just as forcing a child to walk before they're ready can frighten them, the same principle applies to toileting.

The average readiness age varies between 18-30 months, but the normal range can be wide — every child develops at their own pace.

Over the years working in this field, the mistake I encounter most is parents comparing their children with each other. It can be completely normal for one child at kindergarten to learn at age 2 and another at 3.5 — the maturation of sphincter muscles depends on genetics and developmental individuality, not the quality of parenting. Starting hastily, on the other hand, can prolong the process and create a negative association in the child, which can take longer to fix than the original delay.

2

Classification of Readiness Indicators

🧠

Cognitive readiness

Understanding simple instructions, ability to follow a 2-step process, understanding the concept of 'wet/dry.'

💪

Physical readiness

Maturation of sphincter control, ability to take off/put on clothing independently, stable walking.

😊

Emotional readiness

Interest in independence, showing enthusiasm toward the toilet, feeling discomfort about being dirty/wet.

Of the three readiness domains, emotional readiness is the most often overlooked. Physical and cognitive indicators can be objectively observed, but emotional willingness is more subjective and often forms after physical readiness — not understanding this distinction causes parents to feel confused, thinking 'he's ready, but doesn't want to.' In my clinical practice, I don't prioritize any one of these three domains over the others — each is a separate system that needs to mature in its own time.

3

Multidisciplinary Perspective (5 specialist views)

🧠 Psychological perspective

Pressure and punishment can significantly delay toilet training — positive reinforcement is the most effective approach.

✋ Occupational therapist perspective

Motor skills like being able to take off clothing independently and sitting with balance on the toilet are important components of the process.

🌊 Sensory Integration perspective

Some children feel internal body signals (interoceptive sense) weakly — this causes difficulty recognizing the urination/defecation signal.

🗣️ Speech-language perspective

The ability to verbally express a body need (even a single word — 'toilet') significantly eases the process.

🏫 Psychopedagogical perspective

A consistent approach with kindergarten/childcare (the same signal words, the same routine) speeds up the process.

Applying these five perspectives together is especially important at the initial assessment stage — for example, a behavior that appears as the child 'not wanting to' may actually be the weak interoceptive sense described by the sensory specialist, or the motor difficulty noted by the occupational therapist, not simple stubbornness.

4

Process Stages

👀

1. Observation

Tracking the child's readiness signs (2-4 weeks)

🚽

2. Familiarization

Getting familiar with the toilet/potty, establishing a routine

🎉

3. Independent Use

Independent requesting and use, transitioning to nighttime control

The pace of transition between these three stages varies greatly from child to child — some children go through all the steps in a few weeks, others need several months. What matters isn't speed, but that the child shows their own initiative at each stage; a parent's attempt to 'rush' usually prolongs the process instead of speeding it up, because the child begins to perceive the toilet as a source of stress.

Clinical Case: Ulviyya's Story

When working on toilet training, I always remind parents of one principle: this process isn't a race, every child becomes ready according to their own biological clock. Ulviyya was a 2-year-7-month-old girl; her parents became worried after comparing her to peers at kindergarten, since some children had already learned toilet use. Assessment showed that Ulviyya's physical readiness indicators (2-hour dry periods, stable walking) were present, but emotional readiness (interest, willingness) hadn't yet formed. Below I share the concrete documents that came out of her program. This profile is common in clinical practice — physical readiness comes before emotional readiness, so most cases that appear as 'capable, but unwilling' are simply a matter of time, not needing extra pressure. Explaining this distinction to parents also helps them feel more at ease themselves.

Therapeutic Assessment Form

Presenting problemNot showing interest in the toilet, crying during previous attempts

In this form, the 'perpetuating factors' column was especially important in Ulviyya's case — the previous unsuccessful attempt itself had created the problem, not real physical unreadiness. Making this distinction correctly is the key to choosing the right intervention strategy.

Ulviyya's example shows that no matter how short the actual program is (only 8 sessions), most of the real work happens outside the clinic, in the family's daily calm, pressure-free attitude — our role is to build the framework and accompany the family through the process of patience.

Therapist–Patient Dialogue

This short excerpt shows Ulviyya's moment of re-familiarization with the toilet — notice that at no step is she required to do anything, she's simply given the freedom to choose.

T: Ulviyya, this is your special seat, you can sit on it if you'd like, or not, that's fine too.

Ulviyya: (looks from a distance)

T: No problem at all, just look if you'd like.

Ulviyya: (comes closer, puts her hand on the seat)

T: Thank you for trying, that's a big step.

Ulviyya: (smiles, doesn't move away)

Sample Homework

Parents were advised, for two weeks, not to offer the toilet at all, and simply to encourage Ulviyya's initial interest related to the toilet (e.g., watching a parent go to the toilet). It was planned to move to the next step only when she showed interest herself. This 'stepping back' strategy seemed paradoxical to the parents at first — 'what do you mean, do nothing?' — but it was exactly this pause that Ulviyya needed to forget the previous negative association and approach the process again on her own initiative. After five days, Ulviyya herself started talking about the toilet, which was exactly the sign we were waiting for.

Assessment Tool: The Toilet Readiness Checklist

About the test: A structured observation checklist based on American Academy of Pediatrics recommendations — it separately assesses physical, cognitive, and emotional readiness indicators.

Why it is administered: To objectively assess the child's biological readiness and prevent early, forced starting.

Who can administer it: A clinical psychologist, pediatric development specialist, or occupational therapist.

Materials used: The checklist sheet, parent observation notes.

This checklist's greatest benefit is that it makes the concept of 'readiness' concrete for the family — instead of an abstract 'ready/not ready' judgment, tracking each indicator separately makes it possible to see exactly which area still needs time. In Ulviyya's case, this showed the family that the real issue wasn't 'laziness,' but simply emotional readiness lagging one step behind.

Recommendations for Parents

🚫 Don't get angry, don't pressure

Accidents are a normal part of the process — a calm reaction supports long-term success.

👖 Choose comfortable clothing

Easy-to-remove clothing (elastic-waist pants) makes independence easier.

🎯 Note small successes

Every attempt (regardless of outcome) should be praised positively.

9

Risk Assessment and Crisis Protocol

⚠️ Sudden regression — when it's not just a developmental phase

  • Sudden, unexplained loss of a previously acquired toileting skill (especially after age 3+);
  • Blood in urine/stool, painful urination — a sign of medical urgency;
  • Concerning behavior in the genital area (excessive interest/fear) accompanied by regression.
  1. When sudden regression is identified, first investigate whether there's been any change in the family/environment in the last 4-6 weeks (a new sibling, moving, separation, a new caregiver).
  2. If blood/pain is reported, refer immediately for pediatric evaluation — this is outside the scope of psychological work.
  3. If concerning behavior related to the genital area accompanies the regression, observe with extra care, without accusation, and initiate the reporting procedure if necessary.

📌 Topic Summary

  • Toilet training requires the integration of cognitive, physical, and emotional readiness.
  • Starting early or by force can backfire — biological readiness should be awaited.
  • Positive reinforcement and a patient, consistent approach is the most effective method.
  • Accidents are a normal part and should not be punished.
