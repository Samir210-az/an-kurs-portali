Topic 06 · Emotional Regulation

Anxiety Disorders and Fear

A structured 13-session program — working with generalized anxiety, separation anxiety, and specific phobias.

1

Scientific Definition: Childhood Anxiety Disorders

Clinical definition: According to the DSM-5-TR, childhood anxiety disorders are divided into several subtypes: Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD) — excessive, hard-to-control worry across multiple topics, lasting at least 6 months; Separation Anxiety Disorder — anxiety that exceeds age-appropriate levels regarding separation from a primary attachment figure; Specific Phobia — disproportionate, persistent fear of a concrete object/situation. These disorders are linked to the sensitivity of the connection between the amygdala (the brain's danger-signal center) and the prefrontal cortex (the regulation center).
In simple terms: Think of anxiety as an internal 'danger alarm' — it exists to protect us, but sometimes it goes off more often and louder than needed, even when there's no real danger.

Fear and anxiety are different: fear is a reaction to a concrete, present danger (e.g., a barking dog), while anxiety is directed toward uncertain things that might happen in the future.

Over the years I've seen how important it is to distinguish between these two concepts — because the intervention method differs. For a concrete fear (e.g., dogs), gradual exposure is very effective, but in generalized anxiety, the main work happens in the cognitive domain — questioning 'what if' thoughts. An incorrect diagnostic distinction can lead to the wrong intervention plan.

2

Clinical Classification of Symptoms

🧠

Cognitive

Persistent 'what if' thoughts, attempts to control the future, indecisiveness, difficulty concentrating.

💗

Physical (somatic)

Stomachache, muscle tension, fatigue, sleep problems, a racing heartbeat.

🚫

Behavioral

Avoidance, repeatedly seeking reassurance, excessive attachment to the parent, avoiding new situations.

These three categories often mask each other — parents frequently focus only on the physical complaint (stomachache), while cognitive and behavioral components are present behind it too. To see the full picture, each of the three areas must be asked about separately.

3

Multidisciplinary Perspective (5 specialist views)

🧠 Psychological perspective

Intolerance of uncertainty is one of the core drivers of anxiety and a target of cognitive-behavioral therapy.

✋ Occupational therapist perspective

Deep pressure (a heavy blanket, squeezing) and proprioceptive activities help calm the nervous system.

🌊 Sensory Integration perspective

Persistent tension in the body can be linked to 'sensory seeking' or 'sensory sensitivity' — movement and deep pressure can calm the body.

🗣️ Speech-language / Communication perspective

When young children can't put anxiety into words, it shows up as 'body language' — a stomachache or crying.

🏫 Psychopedagogical perspective

An environment with performance pressure (a highly competitive classroom) can intensify anxiety, while structured expectations reduce it.

I apply these five perspectives together at every stage of the program — because anxiety rarely stems from a single domain. In one child, sensory sensitivity may make emotional regulation harder; in another, language difficulty may limit the ability to express feelings, turning anxiety into body language instead.

4

Presentation by Age Group

👶 3-6 years

  • Mainly separation anxiety and specific fears (darkness, monsters)
  • Somatic complaints (stomachache) are more common

🎨 6-11 years

  • Anxiety related to school performance increases
  • Social comparison and friendship anxiety begin to appear

📚 12+ years

  • Generalized future-oriented anxiety becomes predominant
  • Social anxiety and performance anxiety (exams) are the main themes

Considering the age factor matters not just for the form of symptoms, but for which technique will work. At a young age, visual tools (a thermometer, cards) are more effective, while in adolescence, verbal cognitive techniques (questioning thoughts) give better results, since the capacity for abstract thinking is more mature at that age.

It can be hard for a child to put their emotion into words — a visual 'thermometer' fills this gap and helps show how intense the feeling is.

In practice, using these two tools together is most effective — first the child names how big their anxiety is with the thermometer, then calms the body with the breathing technique. This sequence — first naming, then regulating — is based on the neuroscience principle of 'name it to tame it.'

Clinical Case: Vusala's Story

When working with anxiety, I always emphasize one thing to children: the goal isn't to eliminate anxiety completely, but to be able to manage it. Vusala was a 9-year-old girl; her parents reported that she complained of a stomachache every morning before school, and cried when she had to speak in front of the class. Assessment revealed a combination of generalized anxiety and social anxiety elements. Below I share the concrete documents that came out of her program. Mixed presentations like this are common in clinical practice — a child rarely has a single 'pure' type of anxiety; usually several subtypes reinforce each other.

When filling out this form, the most important step is the 'perpetuating factors' column — because often it's not the original cause but the daily reactions that keep the problem going.

Sample Session Protocol

StageSessionNote
Assessment and psychoeducation1-3Anxiety themes mapped, the Fear Thermometer introduced
Cognitive restructuring7'What if' thoughts questioned, balanced alternatives found
Graded exposure ladder8-9Progressed from speaking in front of family to speaking in front of the class

The point I emphasize to specialists whenever I show this chart is this: the sequence between sessions isn't arbitrary — body signals must be recognized first, then regulation tools taught, and only afterward should graded exposure begin. Breaking this sequence risks pushing the child to a level they aren't ready for, too soon.

Therapist–Patient Dialogue

T: Vusala, on a scale from 1 to 10, what does speaking in front of the class feel like to you?

Vusala: It feels like a 9, I'm really scared.

T: I understand. Let's break this into small steps — what could the easiest step be?

Vusala: Maybe first I could say one sentence in front of you.

T: Good idea, let's try it — start whenever you're ready.

Vusala: (takes a deep breath, says one sentence)

Sample Homework

Vusala was asked to apply the 'worry time' technique every day, writing down anxious thoughts that came up during the day and reviewing them at a fixed 10-minute time in the evening. Parents were asked, instead of immediately reassuring her frequent question 'will everything be okay?', to patiently redirect with 'we'll talk about that during worry time.' In the first week this was hard for the family, since offering immediate comfort is a natural parental instinct — but gradually Vusala learned to delay her own worry herself.

Assessment Tool: SCARED (Screen for Child Anxiety Related Disorders)

About the test: SCARED, developed by Boris Birmaher, is a self-report scale with child and parent forms that distinguishes subtypes of anxiety disorders (generalized, separation, social, panic, school phobia).

Why it is administered: To determine which subtype of anxiety predominates and focus the intervention accordingly.

Who can administer it: A clinical psychologist or a specialist trained in anxiety disorders.

Materials used: SCARED child and parent forms, a scoring chart.

Filling out SCARED's child and parent forms separately has great value — often the difference between the two forms itself carries diagnostic information. A child may reveal internal anxiety in their own form that the parent doesn't see, which sharpens the intervention plan.

Recommendations for Parents

🚫 Don't say "don't worry"

Instead, say "I understand you're worried, let's think about it together."

✅ Limit reassurance-seeking

Constantly giving reassurance provides short-term relief but reinforces anxiety in the long run.

🗓️ Protect the routine

A predictable daily rhythm gives a sense of safety.

10

Risk Assessment and Crisis Protocol

⚠️ Panic episodes and the threshold of functional impairment

  • A racing heartbeat and a feeling of being unable to breathe during a panic attack — the first episode always requires ruling out medical causes (asthma, a heart problem);
  • Fear/anxiety completely paralyzing daily functioning (not going to school, not leaving the house);
  • Anxiety escalating into self-harm thoughts ('I can't take it, I want it to end').
  1. At the first panic attack, inform the parent of the importance of a medical evaluation (pediatrician) — a psychological diagnosis is only made after medical causes have been ruled out.
  2. During an acute episode, support with the 4-7-8 breathing technique and grounding, creating a calm, safe environment.
  3. If functional paralysis (not attending school) persists, build a gradual exposure plan together with the school psychologist.
  4. If self-harm thoughts are identified, move directly to risk assessment and inform the parent.

📌 Topic Summary

  • Anxiety is a natural, but sometimes excessively strong, reaction to future uncertainty.
  • Fear is concrete, while anxiety is future-oriented and uncertain.
  • The Fear Thermometer and breathing techniques are the most effective practical tools.
  • Graded exposure is the core method for breaking the avoidance cycle.
