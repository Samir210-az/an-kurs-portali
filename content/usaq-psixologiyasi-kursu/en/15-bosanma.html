Topic 15 · Family Transitions

Working with Children of Divorced Families

A 12-session program — supporting the child's emotional resilience through family transition.

1

Scientific Definition: The Impact of Divorce on the Child

Clinical definition: Research (Amato, Hetherington) shows that it isn't divorce itself, but ongoing conflict between parents, that is the strongest predictor of psychological outcomes for the child. Children can go through various emotional stages during the divorce process — shock/denial, sadness, anger, feelings of guilt (often blaming themselves), and finally adjustment. Age-related differences matter: younger children tend more toward self-blame, while adolescents lean more toward loyalty conflict (pressure to take one parent's side).
In simple terms: For a child, the family is a 'safety island.' Divorce is a change in the shape of this island — it doesn't have to be a bad thing in itself, but the transition period absolutely requires support, especially when parents can keep their tension away from the child.

The strongest protective factor is the child's ability to maintain a healthy relationship with both parents and parents resolving their own conflicts away from the child.

Working in this field over the years, the misconception I encounter most is parents' thinking: 'I won't say anything, to protect my child.' In reality, silence doesn't create emptiness — the child fills the void with the worst-case scenario themselves. That's why an open, age-appropriate, simple explanation is always more protective than silence — the child should know, but shouldn't be overwhelmed with detail.

2

Classification of Emotional Reactions

😢

Sadness and a Sense of Loss

Sadness related to the loss of the family's previous form, sometimes not openly expressed.

😔

Feelings of Guilt

The thought 'I caused this,' especially common in younger children.

⚖️

Loyalty Conflict

Feeling pressure to take one parent's side, conflicting with the desire to remain loyal to both parents.

3

Multidisciplinary Perspective (5 specialist views)

🧠 Psychological perspective

Normalizing the child's emotions and creating a safe space for expressing them is the core of intervention.

✋ Occupational therapist perspective

A structured routine (a consistent schedule in both homes) gives the child a sense of stability and reduces stress.

🌊 Sensory Integration perspective

Stress can manifest as bodily tension — movement and relaxation activities help with emotional regulation.

🗣️ Speech-language perspective

When young children can't verbally express complex emotions, this can come out as behavior or somatic complaints — teaching a simple emotion vocabulary is helpful.

🏫 Psychopedagogical perspective

The school being aware of this transition, and temporarily reducing academic pressure, supports the child.

Applying these five perspectives together is especially important during divorce, because stress can present in various forms — academic decline in some children, somatic complaints (stomachache, headache) in others, and behavioral change in still others. A single specialist can't see this whole spectrum on their own.

4

Reactions by Age Group

👶 3-6 years

  • The risk of self-blame is highest
  • Sensitivity to routine changes, increased separation anxiety

🎨 6-12 years

  • Open expression of anger and sadness, impact on school performance
  • May try to take on a 'bridge' role between parents

📚 12+ years

  • Loyalty conflict is felt most intensely at this age
  • Anxiety about their own future relationships (fear of experiencing the same thing) can develop

Considering the age factor is decisive for how the explanation should be shaped — at a young age, concrete, simple sentences ('this isn't your fault') are enough, while adolescence requires a more complex, respectful dialogue, because at this age the child is already forming their own judgment and may find a simplified explanation 'condescending.'

5

Core Model: Protective and Risk Factors

🛡️ Protective Factors

A healthy relationship with both parents, parents keeping their own conflict away from the child, a stable routine, permission for open emotional expression.

⚠️ Risk Factors

Ongoing inter-parental conflict, using the child as a 'messenger,' badmouthing one parent, absence of stability.

💡 Important note: Research repeatedly shows that it isn't divorce itself, but ongoing, open inter-parental conflict, that does the main harm to the child.

The point I emphasize when showing this chart to every family is this: most of the risk factors are within the parents' control, while the divorce itself is already a fact that has happened. This gives the family power — the message 'we can't undo this, but we can choose how we move forward' turns a feeling of helplessness into a capacity to act.

Clinical Case: Nicat's Story

When working with children of divorced families, I always remember one principle: it isn't the divorce itself that harms children, it's the ongoing war. Nicat was a 7-year-old boy whose parents had formally separated 6 months earlier. Nicat believed he had caused the divorce 'because he was naughty,' and felt he was 'betraying' his mother whenever he saw his father. The Kinetic Family Drawing showed that he drew himself distant from and smaller than his father — reflecting his internal emotional distance. Below I share the concrete documents that came out of his program. Nicat's story shows that feelings a child doesn't put into words often show up in drawing, play, and behavior — our job is to be able to read this symbolic language.

In Nicat's form, the 'core belief' column had two layers — both guilt and loyalty conflict existed at the same time. This is a very common combination, and both need to be addressed separately, directly; one doesn't automatically resolve the other.

Sample Session Protocol

StageSessionNote
Kinetic Family Drawing1Father drawn to the side, small — an indicator of emotional distance
Bibliotherapy — guilt4The feeling of guilt was surfaced and refuted through a character's story
Parent BIFF training6The frequency of arguments in front of the child significantly decreased
Final drawing comparison12In the new drawing, the father was drawn close and smiling

Notice in this chart that parent training (session 6) sits exactly in the middle of the program — this isn't accidental. Work with the child, if inter-parental conflict continues, only gives partial results; so regulating parent communication is a parallel line that reinforces the effect of the work with the child.

Therapist–Patient Dialogue

T: Nicat, you said you sometimes argued with your parents — could this have caused the divorce?

Nicat: I don't know, maybe.

T: Decisions between adults are completely separate from children's behavior — this is a decision two adults made about their own relationship.

Nicat: Really? I thought it was my fault.

T: That's completely true, you didn't do anything wrong.

Nicat: (relaxes) It's good to know that.

Sample Homework

Nicat was asked to hold the small star keychain his mother gave him during every transition (from one home to the other), take 3 deep breaths, and think 'I am safe.' Parents were asked to fully postpone any tense topic in front of the child, and switch to written communication using the BIFF principle. In the first two weeks the parents still slipped back into old habits, but from the third week on, written communication began to become a natural habit.

Assessment Tool: The Kinetic Family Drawing (KFD)

About the test: The Kinetic Family Drawing, developed by Robert Burns and S. Harvard Kaufman, is a projective method requiring the child to draw family members 'doing something,' where the figures' distance and activity reveal relationship dynamics.

Why it is administered: To surface, symbolically and without direct questioning, how the child internally perceives family relationships.

Who can administer it: A clinical psychologist trained in interpreting projective methods.

Materials used: A4 paper, colored pencils, an interpretation protocol.

The KFD's distinction from other projective methods lies in the 'action' element — a static family portrait often shows the picture of 'how things should be,' while the kinetic version reveals the dynamic the child actually perceives, because the child must assign a concrete activity to each figure.

Recommendations for Parents

🚫 Don't make the child a 'messenger'

Using the child to relay messages to the other parent intensifies loyalty conflict.

🗣️ Don't badmouth the other parent

The child is a part of both parents — badmouthing one affects the child's own self-perception.

📅 Keep the routine stable

A routine that's as similar as possible in both homes gives the child a sense of stability.

10

Risk Assessment and Crisis Protocol

⚠️ Child safety in a high-conflict divorce

  • The child being forced into unsupervised contact with a parent facing violence allegations;
  • Signs of parental alienation — the child expressing unfounded, exaggerated hatred toward the other parent;
  • The child taking on a 'mediator' role in inter-parental conflict, expressions of hopelessness/self-harm.
  1. In cases with violence allegations, the therapist never gives a recommendation about the visitation schedule under any circumstance — this is within the court's/social services' authority; document observations objectively.
  2. In suspected parental alienation, remain cautious and unbiased with both parents — listen to the child's story rather than 'confirming' it.
  3. In expressions of hopelessness/self-harm, ask the risk question directly and inform both parents (whichever is safe).
  4. Work with parents separately on setting boundaries to remove the child from the 'mediator' role.

📌 Topic Summary

  • It isn't the divorce itself, but ongoing inter-parental conflict, that does the main harm to the child.
  • Children often experience guilt and loyalty conflict — these should be normalized.
  • A stable routine and a healthy relationship with both parents are the main protective factors.
  • Allowing open, age-appropriate emotional expression increases the child's resilience.
