Topic 03 · Autism Spectrum

Autism Spectrum Disorder and Developmental Delay

Early intervention with ABA (Applied Behavior Analysis) and other evidence-based programs (TEACCH, PECS, ESDM) — from theory to practical application.

1

Scientific Definition: Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD)

Clinical definition: According to the DSM-5-TR, Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by persistent difficulties in two core domains: (1) persistent deficits in social-communicative interaction (social-emotional reciprocity, nonverbal communication, developing and maintaining relationships), and (2) restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior, interests, or activities (stereotyped movements, resistance to change, restricted interests, sensory sensitivity). Symptoms appear during early development and significantly affect daily functioning. ASD is called a "spectrum" because severity ranges widely, from Level 1 (requiring support) to Level 3 (requiring very substantial support).
In simple terms: An autistic child's brain processes the world through a different "operating system" — sounds may seem louder, lights brighter, and social signals more "encoded." This isn't an illness — it's a different way of perceiving. Our goal isn't to "fix" the child, but to support them in their own language.

Developmental delay, unlike ASD, means not progressing at an age-appropriate pace in one or more areas (language, motor, cognitive), and it doesn't necessarily co-occur with ASD.

Over years of building ABA programs, I've become certain of one thing — the greatest progress appears in families where the parent applies the same principles at home. A one-hour clinic session, if not continued at home, has limited impact. That's why parent training sits at the center of every program, not just work with the child.

2

Clinical Classification of Symptoms

🗣️

Social-communicative

Limited eye contact, delayed response to name, lack of pointing, absence of joint attention, difficulty forming friendships.

E.g.: the child doesn't look at the parent to show them something interesting.

🔁

Repetitive behavior/interests

Stereotyped movements (hand-flapping, spinning), rigid attachment to routine, narrow and intense interests, unusual use of objects.

E.g.: severe distress when a usual route is changed.

🌈

Sensory sensitivity

Hyper- (excessive) or hypo- (reduced) reactivity to sound, touch, light, or taste.

E.g.: unable to tolerate the feeling of sand at the beach.

3

Multidisciplinary Perspective (5 specialist views)

🧠 Psychological perspective

ASD is diagnosed through a combination of clinical observation, standardized scales (CARS-2, ADOS-2), and developmental history. Early intervention allows maximum benefit from the brain's plasticity (neuroplasticity).

✋ Occupational therapist perspective

Focuses on daily living skills (dressing, eating, self-care) and fine/gross motor development, targeting functional independence.

🌊 Sensory Integration perspective

The sensory profile is assessed (hyper/hypo reactivity) and a "sensory diet" helps regulate the body — this can be a root cause of behavioral problems.

🗣️ Speech-language / Communication perspective

For nonverbal children, Augmentative and Alternative Communication (AAC) tools — PECS, speech-generating boards — make functional communication possible, even before spoken language.

🏫 Psychopedagogical perspective

A structured, visually supported learning environment (TEACCH principles) increases the child's ability to work independently and eases transitions.

4

Intervention Focus by Age Group

👶 2–4 years (Early intervention)

  • ESDM (Early Start Denver Model) — play-based intervention in natural settings
  • Developing joint attention and imitation skills is the priority
  • Parent training is decisive at this stage

🎨 4–6 years (Preschool)

  • Discrete Trial Training (DTT) and PECS application expand
  • Social skills groups can begin
  • School readiness — work on transition skills

📚 6–12 years (School age)

  • TEACCH structured teaching is integrated into the classroom setting
  • Generalization — applying skills across different environments
  • Social-emotional support alongside academic skills

Core Model: The ABC Principle of ABA

Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA) is based on the principle that every behavior has an antecedent and a consequence. To change a behavior, this triad must first be understood.

A

Antecedent (Trigger)

What happens before the behavior — a task, environment, demand

B

Behavior

The child's concrete, observable response

C

Consequence

What happens after the behavior — increasing or decreasing reinforcement

💡 Example: Antecedent — a demand to sit at the table; Behavior — the child screams and runs away; Consequence — the demand is withdrawn. Here the child learns that screaming helps escape the demand. ABA recognizes this cycle and replaces it by reinforcing an alternative, functional behavior (e.g., pointing to signal "help").

In practice, this triad is the first thing I look for in every referral — because it's not the name of the behavior but its function that determines the treatment plan. The same "screaming" behavior may serve an escape function in one child and an attention-seeking function in another, so the same plan may not work for two different children.

6

Other Evidence-Based Programs

🗂️ TEACCH

Structured teaching — increases the child's independence through visual schedules, clear physical organization, and work systems.

🖼️ PECS

Picture Exchange Communication System — teaches functional communication to nonverbal children through picture cards, consisting of 6 phases.

🎯 ESDM

Early Start Denver Model — an early intervention model built on play and natural interaction, designed for ages 12-48 months.

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent-mediated intervention

Training parents to apply ABA/ESDM techniques in daily routines — significantly increases the durability of outcomes.

Clinical Case: Aytac's Story

When working with an ABA program, I always tell parents one thing upfront: this isn't a "miraculous fix" — it's a systematic, step-by-step process. Aytac was a 3-year-old girl; after starting kindergarten, her teacher reported that she didn't respond to her name and, instead of playing with other children, spent her time spinning a toy car alone in a corner. After a family assessment, Aytac was diagnosed with ASD, and we began an ABA-based early intervention program with her. Below I share the documents that came out of her initial FBA process.

Therapist–Patient Dialogue

T: Aytac, if you give me this card, I'll give you your favorite toy.

Aytac: (looks at the car, then ignores the card)

T: (physically guides her hand, the card is extended) Great! Here you go, take it!

Aytac: (takes the car, makes brief eye contact)

T: You asked so nicely, well done!

Aytac: (starts playing again, this time a little closer to us)

Sample Homework

Parents were advised that instead of immediately handing Aytac something she wanted, they should give her the opportunity to show a card or make a simple gesture — even a few seconds of waiting strengthens functional communication. They were asked to note how many times she used the card over the course of a week.

Assessment Tool: CARS-2 (Childhood Autism Rating Scale)

About the test: The CARS-2, developed by Eric Schopler, is an internationally recognized rating scale based on direct clinical observation, assessing the severity of autism across 15 functional domains.

Why it is administered: To objectively classify the severity of autism spectrum disorder (mild-moderate or severe) and determine the intensity of the intervention plan.

Who can administer it: A clinical psychologist or autism specialist certified in the CARS-2.

Materials used: CARS-2 rating protocol, various toys for observation, record sheet.

Recommendations for Parents

🔁 Stay consistent

When all family members apply the same approach, learning speeds up significantly.

🎉 Celebrate small wins

Every small step forward (a new word, new eye contact) is a big achievement — reinforce it skillfully.

🧩 Ask "why"

There's a need behind every "problem behavior" — instead of punishment, ask "what is this behavior trying to say?"

Risk Assessment and Crisis Protocol

⚠️ Safety boundaries within an ABA program

Within an ABA intervention, two distinct sources of risk must be monitored — the child's own safety and the ethical application of the program:

  • A sharp increase in the frequency/severity of self-injurious behavior (SIB);
  • An attempt to introduce physical punishment, prolonged restraint, or shaming methods into the program (these are prohibited in ethical ABA practice);
  • The child showing persistent signs of stress during the program (sleep disturbance, loss of appetite, regression).
  1. If SIB frequency/severity increases sharply, immediately repeat the Functional Behavior Assessment (FBA) and rule out a medical source of pain.
  2. Under no circumstances apply punitive, painful, or shaming methods — this violates the modern ethical standards of Applied Behavior Analysis (BACB Ethics Code).
  3. If a parent/guardian reports applying such methods at home, immediately teach alternative, positive-reinforcement-based methods.
  4. If persistent signs of stress are detected during the program, slow the pace and simplify demands — "more pressure" is not the solution.

📌 Topic Summary

  • ASD is a spectrum disorder characterized by social-communicative and repetitive-behavior domains.
  • ABA's ABC model is the core tool for understanding the function of behavior.
  • Programs like TEACCH, PECS, and ESDM are evidence-based approaches that complement ABA.
  • Early, consistent, and parent-involved intervention produces the best outcomes.
