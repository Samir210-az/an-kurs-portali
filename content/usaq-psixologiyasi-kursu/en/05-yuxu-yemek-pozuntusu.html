Topic 05 · Core Body Routines

Sleep and Feeding Disorders

A structured 12-session program — a sensory-based, consistent approach from sleep hygiene to working with selective eating (ARFID).

1

Scientific Definition: The Clinical Foundations of Sleep and Feeding Disorders

Clinical definition: According to the DSM-5-TR, Behavioral Insomnia of Childhood is divided into two subtypes: 'sleep-onset association type' (the child can only fall asleep under certain conditions — e.g., in a parent's arms) and 'limit-setting type' (resistance and stalling behaviors at bedtime). In feeding, ARFID (Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder) is a feeding disorder characterized by significant restriction of caloric/food variety, lack of weight gain, or nutritional deficiency, that is not related to body image concerns.
In simple terms: Sleep and eating are two fundamental routines that power the body's 'core operating system.' If a child struggles with one of these, it isn't 'being spoiled' — it's often connected to the sensory system, regulation ability, or learned associations. For example, the difference in texture between chicken and potato can feel 'horrifying' to some children — this isn't 'being moody,' it's a genuine sensory experience.

These two areas are frequently interconnected: a tired child may become more selective with food, and selective eating can affect sleep quality — so the program examines both in parallel.

Over the years of working on these two areas separately, I've often seen the outcome remain incomplete. In a sleep-deprived child, food selectivity intensifies further, because a tired nervous system isn't open to new sensory experiences. That's why at our center we track both areas within the same program framework, in parallel — full stabilization in one is rarely possible without progress in the other.

Clinical Classification of Symptoms

🌙

Difficulty falling asleep

Resistance to going to bed, only falling asleep with a parent/in arms, a long sleep-onset latency (30+ minutes).

🌃

Night awakenings

Frequent waking at night, needing parental intervention to fall back asleep, night terrors.

🍽️

Selective/restricted eating

A very narrow food range (fewer than 10 food types), refusal to try new foods, texture/taste sensitivity.

3

Multidisciplinary Perspective (5 specialist views)

🧠 Psychological perspective

Bedtime anxiety or separation anxiety can be a core psychological cause of sleep problems — the approach must be gradual and pressure-free.

✋ Occupational therapist perspective

The oral-motor coordination needed for eating (chewing, swallowing) is assessed — sometimes 'selectivity' is actually a motor difficulty.

🌊 Sensory Integration perspective

Sensitivity to touch, taste, and smell is the most common cause of selective eating — sensory tolerance is built gradually, in a playful format.

🗣️ Speech-language perspective

Oral-motor skills (lip closure, tongue movement) affect both speech and eating — both areas are assessed in parallel.

🏫 Psychopedagogical perspective

Having bedtime and mealtime routines at consistent times in the daily schedule increases the child's sense of predictability and cooperation.

4

Differences by Age Group

👶 1-3 years

  • Sleep associations (pacifier, being held) are the most common problem
  • 'Neophobia' (fear of novelty) toward new foods is normal at this age, but its severity should be monitored

🎨 3-6 years

  • Resistance to bedtime and 'stalling' behaviors increase
  • A narrowing of food variety (only 5-10 'safe' foods) may be observed

📚 6-12 years

  • Night terrors and nightmares become more noticeable
  • Eating in social contexts (school cafeteria) can create additional difficulty

In practice, I never overlook the age factor — fear of novelty in a 2-year-old and established selectivity in an 8-year-old require different intervention paces. At a young age, patient waiting is often enough; at an older age, a structured, step-by-step program is needed.

5

Core Model: The SOS Feeding Approach and Sleep Hygiene

The most widely used evidence-based model for working with selective eating is the Sequential Oral Sensory (SOS) approach, which provides a pressure-free, staged approach to new foods.

👀

1. Looking/Tolerating

The food is on the table, the child isn't required to touch it

👆

2. Touching/Smelling

Touching the food by hand, smelling it — no tasting yet

👅

3. Tasting/Eating

Tasting a small amount, gradually swallowing

💡 Important note: There is NO 'forcing' between these stages — the child progresses at their own pace; pressure can have the opposite effect and cause even more avoidance of the food.

In practice, I always describe these three stages to parents as a physical staircase — each step depends on the previous one, and rushing past isn't possible. A child may stay at the 'looking' stage for weeks — this is normal, and rushing has the opposite effect.

Clinical Case: Zohra's Story

When working on sleep and feeding problems, I always remind parents of this: it isn't a 'parenting mistake' — it's often the result of the sensory system and learned associations. Zohra was a 4-year-old girl; her family reported that she accepted only 6 types of food and had to be in her mother's arms to fall asleep. Both difficulties reinforced each other — a tired child ate more selectively, and selective eating negatively affected sleep quality. Below I share the concrete documents that came out of her program. The biggest mistake in cases like this is rushing — neither sleep nor eating is resolved overnight, both are a process made up of small steps that continues over weeks.

Therapist–Patient Dialogue

T: Zohra, I've brought something new that looks like a potato cracker, you can just look at it if you'd like.

Zohra: (looks from a distance) What is this?

T: It's cooked potato, it's the same color as the cracker you like.

Zohra: (comes closer, smells it) The smell is different.

T: No problem at all, just smelling it is enough for today.

Zohra: (touches it with one finger) That's enough.

Sample Homework

Parents were advised to place a new food similar in appearance next to Zohra's familiar food at dinner each evening, without pressure — just being on the table is enough, touching or tasting isn't required. They were asked to note over the course of a week which level (looking/smelling/touching) Zohra approached the new food with. Tasks like this often seem 'too small' to parents, but it's exactly this smallness that increases their chance of success — high expectations always backfire.

Assessment Tool: The Sensory Profile Questionnaire

About the test: The Sensory Profile (Winnie Dunn) is a standardized questionnaire that assesses, based on parent report, how a child responds in daily life to sensory input such as sound, touch, taste, and movement.

Why it is administered: To identify the sensory sensitivity patterns (hyper/hypo reactivity) behind selective eating and tailor the intervention accordingly.

Who can administer it: An occupational therapist or a specialist certified in sensory integration.

Materials used: Sensory Profile questionnaire sheet, record chart.

The results of the Sensory Profile are usually the first objective document that lets parents hear 'this wasn't just a parenting issue' — this both reduces the family's self-blame and directs the intervention in the right direction.

Recommendations for Parents

🕐 Keep a consistent schedule

Having bedtime and mealtimes at the same time every day regulates the body clock.

🚫 Don't turn food into a reward/punishment tool

Approaches like 'if you eat this, you'll get dessert' make the relationship with food harder in the long term.

😌 Stay calm

Reacting with anxiety to a child's refusal to eat or sleep can reinforce the behavior — a calm, neutral approach is more effective.

Risk Assessment and Crisis Protocol

⚠️ Signs requiring urgent medical evaluation

In the sleep and feeding disorders module, the therapist's role is psychological support, but certain signs require immediate medical evaluation and fall outside the scope of psychological work:

  • Related to feeding: acute weight loss, rushing to the bathroom after eating, systematic avoidance of food intake — these require immediate referral to a pediatrician/child psychiatrist;
  • Related to sleep: risk of self-injury during sleepwalking (proximity to a window, stairs), signs of breathing pauses (suspected sleep apnea).
  1. When dangerous signs related to food are identified, the therapist does not give a specific diet or weight recommendation — this is a medical domain; refer immediately for pediatric/psychiatric evaluation.
  2. In cases of sleepwalking, ensure the physical safety of the home environment (window locks, stair gates) — this is a matter for direct work with the parent.
  3. If a breathing disorder is suspected, refer to a somnologist/pulmonologist.
  4. Address the emotional component (anxiety, body-image concerns) with parallel psychological support, but this does not replace medical monitoring.

📌 Topic Summary

  • Sleep and feeding difficulties are frequently interconnected and should be addressed in parallel.
  • The SOS approach provides a gradual, pressure-free path for selective eating.
  • Sensory sensitivity is often the root cause behind both selective eating and sleep resistance.
  • A calm, consistent parental approach is more effective than pressure or reward-based tactics.
