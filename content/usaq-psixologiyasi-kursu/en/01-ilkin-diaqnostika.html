Topic 01 · Assessment

Initial Diagnostic Assessment and Drawing Analysis

Principles of early assessment in children and projective drawing analysis methods — a complete roadmap from clinical observation to the final report.

1

Scientific Definition: Developmental Screening and Projective Assessment

Clinical definition: Initial diagnostic assessment (developmental screening) is a structured process that compares a child's age-appropriate developmental indicators — in cognitive, language, motor, social-emotional, and self-care domains — against normative benchmarks. This process combines history-taking, direct observation, the application of standardized tools (Denver II, the Gesell scale, etc.), and projective techniques — including drawing analysis. Drawing analysis is grounded in the psychodynamic approach: the child transfers, in symbolic form onto paper, inner conflicts, emotional states, and self-perception that they cannot yet fully express in words (Machover's Draw-A-Person Test, Buck's House-Tree-Person Test).
In simple terms: A young child already has a language they can't yet fully "speak" in words — and that language is play and drawing. Assessment is, in essence, the process of "translation" we use to understand that language: we observe the child, play with them, "read" their drawing, and combine everything we hear from the parent into a complete picture.

The goal of assessment is not to attach a label — it is to precisely identify the child's strengths and the areas where support is needed, and to build an individual development plan accordingly.

Over years of conducting assessments, there's one principle I remind myself of every single time — the first impression from the initial meeting is never the final word. A child does not adapt quickly to an unfamiliar setting, so chasing an "instant result" from a single visit is both scientifically flawed and unfair to the family. The most accurate picture usually emerges at the point where several different methods (interview, observation, drawing, standardized testing) converge.

2

Classification of Assessment Stages

📋

History taking

A structured interview covering the pregnancy, birth, early developmental milestones, family history, and parental concerns.

👁️

Direct observation

Recording the child's attention, communication, motor skills, and emotional reactions in a free-play setting.

🖍️

Projective methods

Reading the child's inner world in symbolic terms through tools such as drawing, toy play, and story completion.

3

Multidisciplinary Perspective

🧠 Psychological perspective

Assessment is not a one-time "test" — it is a process: the first impression must always be confirmed or reconsidered in subsequent sessions. A child's anxiety can distort the outcome, so building trust is the first step.

✋ Occupational therapy / Sensory integration perspective

Pencil grip, line pressure, and the size and placement of the figure on the page all provide valuable information about fine motor skills and sensory processing. Lines that are too faint or too heavily pressed may indicate sensory regulation difficulty.

🗣️ Speech-language / Communication perspective

A child's self-talk during drawing, naming of figures, and question-and-answer responses offer an additional window into assessing receptive and expressive speech.

🏫 Psychopedagogical perspective

How a child approaches a task (patiently, hastily, giving up) is an early indicator of what kind of support they may need in a future educational setting.

4

Diagnostic Focus by Age Group

👶 0–3 years

  • Mainly observation and parent interview (Denver II)
  • Visual-motor coordination, looking at named objects, first words
  • Drawing does not yet exist — the "scribbling" stage is noted

🎨 3–6 years

  • The House-Tree-Person drawing can be applied
  • Symbolic and role play observation is the primary tool
  • Attention, sitting time, and instruction-following are assessed

📚 6–12 years

  • The Kinetic Family Drawing yields more precise results
  • Standardized cognitive tests (IQ, Bender-Gestalt) are applied
  • Questions related to school performance are added

Core Model: Criteria for Reading Drawings

A drawing is never interpreted based on a single feature — it is always assessed within the full context, together with the child's age and other observations.

CriterionWhat is observedPossible meaning (in context)
Figure sizeHow much of the page it occupiesVery small — shyness/low self-confidence; very large — impulsivity
Line pressureHow hard the pencil presses on the paperVery light — withdrawal; very heavy — tension, aggressive energy
PlacementThe figure's position on the pageBottom corner — low self-esteem; center — stability
Number of detailsHands, fingers, facial featuresVery few details — possible developmental delay; excessive detail — perfectionism
Color choiceNumber and tone of colors usedSingle color/dark tones — emotional constriction (interpreted only alongside other indicators)
💡 Important note: Drawing analysis alone does not establish a diagnosis — it is a supplementary source that strengthens or raises a hypothesis, and is always assessed together with other methods.

In practice, this is how I apply it: I don't immediately assign meaning to every feature that catches my attention in a drawing — instead, I note it as a "question mark" and track whether it reappears in later sessions (observation, parent interview). Only when the same signal emerges from several sources do I include that finding in the report — this is the most reliable way to guard against over-interpretation.

Clinical Case: Sabina's Story

Every time I've applied this process at our center, I become certain of the same truth once again — every child opens up at their own pace, and rushing never helps. Sabina was a 4-year-old girl; when her parents brought her to us, their main concerns centered on two issues: her speech being somewhat behind her peers, and remaining quiet and on the sidelines for long periods in new environments. Below I share the concrete documents that emerged from her initial diagnostic process — in the exact format we use in real clinical practice.

T: You like the blocks? I'd love to build with you, if you'd like.

Sabina: (smiles, adds another block)

Sample Homework

Sabina's parents were asked to play a 10-minute "naming game" with her every evening — pointing to household objects together and naming them, waiting five seconds for Sabina to respond, and instead of correcting her right away, affirming her attempt. A week later, they were asked to note how many new words she had used.

Assessment Tool: Denver II Developmental Screening Test

About the test: The Denver II, created in 1992 by William Frankenburg, is an internationally recognized screening tool that compares a child's development across four domains (personal-social, fine motor, language, gross motor) in children aged 0–6 against normative benchmarks.

Why it is administered: To determine early on whether a child's development aligns with age norms, and to refer for further assessment if needed.

Who can administer it: A Denver II-certified psychologist, pediatrician, or developmental specialist.

Materials used: Test chart, small cubes, red yarn, bell, pencil, paper, simple picture cards.

Recommendations for Parents

🎨 Keep drawing natural

Instead of saying "draw something nice" before the session, simply say "draw whatever you'd like" — don't try to control the outcome.

📝 Don't hide your concerns

Share your real observations during the interview — saying "I'm a bit worried that..." enriches the assessment, while hiding it only slows the process down.

⏳ Don't rely on a single session

A clear picture usually forms after 2–3 visits — don't rush to worry based on the first impression.

Risk Assessment and Crisis Protocol

⚠️ Warning signs that may surface during the diagnostic process

The initial assessment stage is often where the first, not-yet-understood signs appear. During drawing analysis and observation, the following require particular attention:

  • Repeated sexual content or age-inappropriate details in drawings;
  • Marks of injury on the body that don't match the child's account;
  • Sudden regression — the abrupt loss of a previously acquired skill, especially within the last few weeks;
  • The child expressing fear of a specific person, or a change in behavior following contact with that person.
  1. If a concerning detail appears in a drawing, don't accuse right away — ask a simple, open question: "Who's in this picture, what's happening here?"
  2. If a bodily injury is found, note the parent's explanation, but also have an additional conversation with the child alone, if it is safe to do so.
  3. If there is reasonable suspicion, pause the assessment and immediately begin the reporting procedure to the appropriate child protection authority.
  4. Document all observations in objective language, without adding interpretation — this record may carry legal significance.

📌 Topic Summary

  • Initial assessment combines history-taking, observation, and projective methods (drawing).
  • Drawing analysis should not stand alone — it must be interpreted together with other tools.
  • Choosing methods suited to the age group increases the reliability of results.
  • The ultimate goal of assessment is not labeling, but building an individual support plan.
